---
title: You only get to watch one person think
summary: You get the full recording of your own reasoning and a highlight reel of everyone else's. There is good evidence that this makes us wrong about each other in one specific direction, and that being wrong about it is quietly killing people.
date: "2026-07-25"
---

You have watched exactly one person think. Your own reasoning is available to you
in full, including the parts you would never say out loud. The idea you dropped.
The sentence you nearly sent. The real reason you were short with someone on
Tuesday, which was not the reason you gave them.

Everyone else arrives finished. You get their output. Decisions already made,
sentences already chosen, the tone they settled on instead of the four they tried
first. You have never once watched another person hesitate on the inside.

I think almost everything that goes wrong between people comes out of that one
gap, and I think it is worse than most of us admit, because it does not feel like
a limitation. It feels like having the facts.

Here is the part that convinced me it is real and not just a nice idea.

In 2018 a few researchers ran a very plain experiment. They had strangers talk to
each other, then asked each person two questions. How much did you like them. How
much do you think they liked you. The second number came back lower than the
truth, over and over. People walked out of a good conversation quietly certain it
had gone worse than it did. They called it the liking gap, and it has held up
since in groups, in teams, online, between people speaking their second language,
and in children as young as five.

Sit with the shape of that for a second. Two people talk. Both walk away thinking
they were the one who was tolerated. Both are wrong. Both are wrong in the same
direction, at the same time, about each other.

The reason is not that people are insecure, or not only that. It is that you were
listening to your own commentary the entire time. Every clumsy phrase, every
moment you thought you were boring, the joke that did not land. That track is
deafening and it belongs to you alone. They did not hear any of it. They got the
version of you that came out of your mouth, which was fine, and they were busy
listening to their own version of the same track.

So the warmth in the room is real, and both people are standing in it thinking
they snuck in.

Once you see it in conversations you start seeing it everywhere else. Because you
have the recording of your own process, you grade yourself on it. You know you
meant well. You know what kind of week it was. When you are late you know about
the phone call. When someone else is late you have the fact that they were late,
and nothing else, so that is what you use. You judge yourself on intent and other
people on impact, and from the inside that does not feel unfair at all.

It also produces one strange symptom worth naming. Most people quietly believe
they are more complicated than the people around them. Not better. More going on
under the surface. Ask carefully and nearly everyone reports it, which is the
tell, because it cannot be true of all of us at once but it is felt by all of us
at once. The reason is not vanity. Each of us has exactly one interior available
for inspection, and it is very loud, and every other one is silent.

When you feel like the only person carrying something complicated, the honest
reading is not that you are unusual. It is that you are the only one whose inside
you can hear.

I would leave it there as an interesting thought if the bill were not so large.

Someone went and counted. In 1990, three percent of American men said they had no
close friends. By 2021 it was fifteen. Over the same stretch, the share with six
or more close friends fell from about fifty five percent to twenty seven. That is
not a mood or a vibe. That is half a generation of men who used to have people and
now do not. Last year the World Health Organization put numbers on the global
version and landed on one in six people affected by loneliness, and roughly eight
hundred and seventy one thousand deaths a year associated with it. That is about a
hundred people an hour.

I am wary of treating a statistic as an argument, so I will say plainly what I
think the connection is. Nobody in those numbers decided to be alone. Almost
everyone in them walked out of rooms where they were liked, believing they were
not, and eventually stopped going into rooms.

Relationships are where the gap stops being interesting and starts costing you
something you cannot buy back.

Most arguments are not about the thing they are about. Someone who tells you that
you never listen is rarely making a claim about listening, which they could not
measure anyway. They are asking to be treated as a process instead of a result.
They want the version of attention you give yourself automatically. Answer the
literal accusation and you will win, and you will have lost, and you will be
genuinely confused about how both of those happened at once.

This is also where trust turns out to be structural instead of sentimental. Trust
is what you extend before the evidence arrives. That sounds reckless until you
notice the alternative does not work. People do not show you their interior unless
you have already assumed they have one. Require proof first and you get the
guarded version, which is the version people hand to strangers, and then you read
that as confirmation. The loop closes and you never learn you were wrong.
Suspicion cannot be disproved from the inside, and that is exactly why it feels
like wisdom.

What I have come to think is that empathy as a feeling is close to useless. It
arrives when it wants to, usually for people who were easy to like anyway, which
is when it is least needed. As a practice it is more reliable and much less
pleasant, because it is an assumption you act on without waiting to feel it. This
person has as much going on as I do. I am seeing almost none of it. I am going to
behave as though the part I cannot see is real.

That is not the same as excusing anyone. You can hold a person to the result and
still credit them the process. Most of us do one or the other. We either forgive
everything because they meant well, which teaches nobody anything, or we treat the
output as the whole person, which is accurate about the moment and wrong about the
human. Doing both at once is harder, and is most of what I think growing up
actually turns out to be.

None of this makes you good, and I am suspicious of anyone who frames it that
way. The real argument is narrower and more selfish.

If you spend long enough treating people as their outputs you will be right a
great deal. You will correctly identify who was careless, who was late, who said
the stupid thing. Your record will be excellent. And you will find yourself in a
smaller and smaller room, because the standard you are holding people to is one
that nobody, including you, survives being measured by from the outside.

Being right is available to almost anyone willing to be alone. The harder thing is
to keep assuming there is something behind the part you can see, with no way to
check, no guarantee, and no credit for it.

I do not always manage it. I have never once regretted the times I did.

Two things worth doing this week, if any of this landed. Tell someone you like
them, plainly, with no joke attached to soften it, because the evidence says they
do not know. And the next time you are certain you understand why someone did
something, notice that you are working from the outside of them and the inside of
yourself, and that you would never accept that trade in reverse.
