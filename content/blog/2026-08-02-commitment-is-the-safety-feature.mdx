I bought the bike ten days ago. A friend took delivery of it, and I collected
it from his garage the same day it turned up.

Today I rode it properly for the first time. Bike park, flow trail, about an
hour, and then the jump line at the end, because by that point I had stopped
being sensible.

I should be clear about where I am starting from, because the rest of this
will make no sense otherwise. I have been on a mountain bike a couple of times
in my life, both times on somebody else's. This is the first time the bike was
mine and the first time I have done the sport properly, so everything below is
a beginner's hour, written down while it is still strange.

That is the reason for writing it now. In a month I will have normalised all
of it and it will stop being visible.

## The bike is a filter, not a vehicle

The first thing that is obvious in about ninety seconds: a full-suspension
bike is not a hardtail with more comfort. It changes what information reaches
you.

A rigid frame transmits everything. Every root, every square edge, every bit
of braking chatter arrives at your hands and your spine at full amplitude, and
you spend real attention filtering it. Suspension eats the high-frequency part
and passes the low-frequency part through. The rocks stop being individually
interesting. What you feel instead is the *shape of the trail*, the
compression into a berm, the unloading over a crest, the long slow change in
grade.

The odd consequence is that the bike is not making the ride easier so much as
making it **legible**. It is a low-pass filter on terrain, and once the noise
is gone there is a signal underneath that I did not previously know was there.

I did not expect the upgrade to be an upgrade in *perception*. I expected it
to be an upgrade in comfort.

## Speed is stability, which is a terrible thing to be told

Every piece of advice from every person on that hill was some version of "you
were going too slow."

This is true and it is genuinely infuriating, because it inverts the control
you reach for when you are frightened. Something feels sketchy, so you brake.
You brake, so you lose the gyroscopic stability and the suspension stops
working in its intended range and the front wheel starts tracking every
deflection instead of rolling over it, so it feels sketchier, so you brake
more.

The loop is tight and it converges on you stopped, sideways, in a berm, which
is where I spent a memorable fraction of that hour.

The fix is not bravery. The fix is that braking has a *place*, before the
corner, in a straight line, while the bike is upright and the tyres have
nothing else to do. Once you are in the turn, the tyres are already spending
their grip on turning and there is none spare to spend on slowing down. Ask
for both and you get neither.

I understood that sentence perfectly well as a sentence. Understanding it as a
sentence bought me approximately nothing.

## The jump line

Then the jumps, which are the reason for the whole post.

Here is what nobody quite conveys until you are looking down a takeoff. A
tabletop is built so that the lip does the work. You do not need to launch
yourself. You need to arrive at a reasonable speed, stay loose, let the ramp
convert your horizontal motion into vertical motion, and then simply not
interfere.

Not interfering turns out to be extremely difficult.

Because what your body wants to do (what mine wanted to do, badly, every
single time) is one of two things. Either grab a fistful of brake on the face
of the jump, or tense up and pull back on the bars. Both feel like caution.
Both are the actual danger. Braking on the lip kills the speed you needed to
clear the gap and drops you on the flat top or, worse, the far edge. Pulling
back unweights the front, the bike rotates, and you land nose-up and
unbalanced on a downslope.

The safe thing and the frightening thing are the same thing. You have to
arrive at the lip having already decided, stay relaxed through a moment where
every alarm in your nervous system is going off, and let the ramp do what
ramps do.

I got a few of them right. Real air, brief, and then the specific quiet of a
clean landing where the suspension absorbs it and you are just *riding* again
on the other side, having done nothing at all.

The rest I chickened out on somewhere in the last two metres, which produced
exactly the ugly, unsettled landings the chickening out was meant to avoid.

## What I actually took from it

I spend my working life on problems where thinking harder is the entire
method. You reason about it, you form a hypothesis, you instrument, you
narrow, you fix the root cause. Deliberation is the tool and more deliberation
is more tool.

This is not that. On a jump face there is no time to deliberate and
deliberation is affirmatively harmful: the hesitation *is* the failure mode.
The decision has to be made early and then executed by a part of me that does
not consult me. The only way to install that part is repetition, at a cost of
some number of bad landings, and there is no shortcut where I read enough
about it beforehand.

I found that genuinely uncomfortable, in a way I am still turning over. I am
very used to being able to think my way to competence. Here the bottleneck is
somewhere below thinking entirely, and the only available input is showing up
again.

The other thing about today is how little the anticipation resembled it.
Exhilarating in the boring literal sense: I came home wired, filthy, with a
bruise I cannot account for, and I have been thinking about the good ones ever
since instead of the ones I bailed on.

Going back this weekend. One hour is not a basis for any opinion I have just
offered, which is precisely why I want the next ten. I intend to be going too
slow considerably less often.
